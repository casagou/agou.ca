How I work

I'm an engineer. I start with the problem, not the ideology. I measure it, fix it, and report the result. Below are the four things neighbours raise with me most, what I'll push for, and how you'll know if it worked. Two supporting sections follow: transport and BC Ferries, and responsible spending.

I'm the Conservative Party of BC's nominee in Victoria–Beacon Hill and support the party's plan. The party's platform page says "coming soon" (checked 1 Oct 2026), so every proposal on this page says where it comes from:

  • 2024 party platform: proposed in the Conservative Party of BC's October 2024 platform. Not yet confirmed for this election.
  • Party, 27 Sep 2026: the party's pledge of no new taxes and no tax increases, the current party commitment this page relies on.
  • My position: mine. I'll advocate for it within the party.

1. Health care you can get

  • Cut doctors' paperwork so every clinic can take more patients (my position; the 2024 party platform also proposed reducing doctors' administrative burden).
  • Add evening and weekend clinic hours on the South Island (my position).
  • Use publicly paid surgeries and scans at non-government clinics to shorten waits (2024 party platform, p. 54).
  • Vote for staffed medical beds, and for paying hospitals for the patients they treat rather than through a block grant (my position, scorecard line 8).
  • Vote to pay for extra operating-room time, public and non-government (my position, scorecard line 9).

What changes for you: you get a family doctor sooner, and you wait less for a hospital bed, a scan or a planned surgery.

How you'll know: scorecard lines 8, 9 and 10 (the emergency wait for a bed, planned-surgery waits and the wait for a family doctor), every quarter, within 30 days of quarter-end. I'll also publish how many people are waiting in each Victoria-area care network, and for how long.

Why: Health care has been managed, not fixed. Since 2018 the Province has run a Primary Care Strategy and committed more than $672 million to it for 2025-26 alone (B.C. Ministry of Health, Aug 2025). About 1 in 4 British Columbians (24.7%) had no family doctor or nurse practitioner in June 2025, and 68.6% of the primary-care positions the Province had approved were filled in 2024/25 (Ministry of Health records, via The Canada Report, 16 Jul 2026). Doctors' pay, clinic rules and paperwork are set by the Province.

How the extra appointments would be delivered:

  • Staff: extra hours need staff first. The 2024 party platform proposed working with physicians, nurse practitioners, nurses and physician assistants to see more patients per practice, and expanding the programs that fill positions in high-need communities (2024 party platform, p. 55 and p. 58).
  • Clinic capacity: long-term contracts with publicly funded non-government clinics, so they can buy equipment and recruit staff (2024 party platform, p. 54).
  • Less administration: modern tools that cut paperwork, and a review to find administrative savings and move the money to front-line staffing (2024 party platform, p. 61).

What shortens waits: publishing the lists is the check, not the fix. The fixes are staffed beds, paying hospitals for the patients they treat, and extra operating-room time.

Cost: cutting paperwork is a rule change, not a new program. I'll publish a local cost note once the party releases updated 2026 figures. Until then I won't invent a number.

Tracked on the scorecard: 8. Emergency wait for a bed · 9. Time to planned surgery · 10. A family doctor

2. Homes people can afford

  • Approve homes in months, not years: 6 months for a rezoning and development permit, 3 months for a building permit. If city hall doesn't give a clear yes or no in time, the Province issues the permits (2024 party platform, p. 20).
  • Lower the fees and paperwork that add to the cost of every new home (my position).
  • Income-tax relief on housing costs (2024 party platform, p. 19): it starts at $1,500 a month of eligible housing costs (rent, mortgage interest and strata fees) and rises by $500 a year toward $3,000. Your eligible housing costs set how much provincial income tax you're relieved of. It is not a monthly payment.

What changes for you: more homes get finished, including family-sized ones, which eases rents over time; and, under the 2024 proposal, less provincial income tax if you pay rent, mortgage interest or strata fees.

How you'll know: scorecard line 3 (net new homes against Victoria's housing target, and how many have three or more bedrooms; annual, with building-permit counts every quarter) and line 4 (rental vacancy, with three-bedroom units shown separately; annual, published each December, with completions as the quarterly stand-in). I'll also publish permit times for every municipality in the region, as a check on approvals.

Why: Homes got more expensive while fewer got built. The average two-bedroom rent in the Victoria area was $2,120 in 2025, up 5.1% in a year (CMHC Rental Market Report, Fall 2025). Housing starts in B.C. were down 9.3% this year to July (B.C. First Quarterly Report, Sept 2026). Much of what slows and adds cost to new homes is set by the Province.

A permit is not a home: permit times alone don't show whether housing got more affordable. Faster approvals count when they turn into finished homes, lower rents and more family-sized units, so I'll judge them on lines 3 and 4. In 2025 the Victoria-area vacancy rate rose to 3.3%, the highest since 1999, and CMHC said vacancy rose significantly downtown and in Saanich, largely because of new rental supply there (CMHC, Fall 2025). Family-sized rentals are still the scarce stock.

The 2024 tax relief in detail: beginning with Budget 2026, the platform would have exempted $1,500 a month of rent, mortgage interest and strata fees from B.C. income tax, with the exempt amount rising by $500 a year until it reached $3,000. The relief equals B.C.'s base tax rate of 5.06% multiplied by eligible housing costs. The platform put the saving at up to $1,800 a year at the full level (2024 platform, p. 4), and the party's 2024 costing at about $900 million in Budget 2026 (2024 costing appendix, Oct 2024). The party has not yet published a 2026 version.

Example (illustration only, from the 2024 platform's figures): a household with at least $1,500 a month of eligible housing costs: $1,500 × 12 months × 5.06% ≈ $911 less B.C. income tax in the first year. At the full $3,000 a month: $3,000 × 12 × 5.06% ≈ $1,822 (the platform said "up to $1,800"). Relief depends on how much B.C. income tax you owe.

Trade-offs and who decides: faster approvals mean less municipal control, and the tax relief lowers provincial revenue. The Province sets the zoning floor, the building code and tenancy law; cities issue the permits (What the Province controls).

Tracked on the scorecard: 3. New homes, and what kind · 4. Finding a rental

3. Cost of living

  • Restore the lowest provincial income-tax rate to 5.06% (my position, scorecard line 1).
  • Restore inflation indexing (my position, scorecard line 1).
  • Keep the enhanced B.C. tax reduction credit, so lower-income taxpayers aren't asked to fund the reversal (my position, scorecard line 1).
  • Publish a verifiable, line-by-line list of government savings, with new extra spending matched by a cut somewhere else (my position, scorecard line 2).
  • Vote no on any bill that raises a provincial tax rate or creates a new tax (my position, scorecard promise 2; the party made the same pledge on 27 Sep 2026).
  • No PST on affordable used cars (2024 party platform, p. 109).
  • Ferry fares: consult commuters and other frequent users on a monthly flat-fee program or other measures (2024 party platform; see Transport and BC Ferries below).

Timing and funding: the year each tax change takes effect, and how it is paid for, follow the party's published fiscal plan. I won't print a year the party hasn't set.

What changes for you: less provincial income tax once the lowest rate and indexing are restored, with lower-income taxpayers protected by the credit; and a public list to check every claimed saving. How much depends on your income and credits.

How you'll know: scorecard line 1 (the rate, indexing and the credit; annual, from the B.C. tax tables and the budget) and line 2 (the savings list; every quarter, from the government's Quarterly Reports).

Why: Government made life more expensive. For 2026 the lowest provincial income-tax rate went from 5.06% to 5.60%, on the first $50,363 of taxable income, and indexing of tax brackets and credits is paused for 2027 through 2030 (B.C. Ministry of Finance, Budget 2026 tax changes).

Effects differ between households: the same budget raised the maximum B.C. tax reduction credit by $115, to $690, and raised the rate used for the basic personal tax credits to 5.60% (same source). The Province says the average taxpayer pays $76 more in 2026, while more than 40% of taxpayers come out ahead once the higher tax reduction credit is counted (Budget 2026 highlights). That is why I'd keep the credit when the rate comes back down.

A balanced budget is not a saving on your next pay cheque. It is how you can check that these commitments are paid for. See Responsible spending below.

Cost: the party has not published a cost for restoring the rate and indexing, and I won't print a personal figure before it does. The party's 2024 costing put its drivers' package, which includes no PST on affordable used cars, at about $60 million from 2026/27 (2024 costing appendix, Oct 2024).

Tracked on the scorecard: 1. Tax on your first dollars · 2. A public savings list

4. A downtown that works

  • Treatment: more staffed detox and treatment beds on the South Island, with wait times published monthly (my position). Secure care for people with severe addiction who are a danger to themselves or others, with medical and legal safeguards (my position; the 2024 party platform also proposed involuntary treatment legislation, p. 79).
  • Housing: move people from tents into housing with support, and report how many are still housed after 12 months (my position; the 2024 party platform also proposed moving campers into support services, p. 5).
  • Safety: add sheriffs and judges so cases get to trial faster. The courts, prosecution and sheriffs are provincial, and I'll vote to fund them. Bail is federal law, so for repeat violent offenders I'll press Ottawa for amendments (my position; the 2024 party platform also proposed more sheriffs and judges).
  • Balance: spread health services across the region instead of concentrating them on a few downtown blocks, as the Downtown Victoria Business Association asks (my position).
  • Small business: cut the small-business tax from 2% to 1%, and work with cities to publish business permit wait times (2024 party platform, p. 11).

What changes for you: a downtown where people get help and businesses stay open, and where you feel safe walking on Pandora.

How you'll know: scorecard lines 5, 6, 7 and 12 (people sleeping outside, toxic-drug deaths, crime in the city including repeat violent offences downtown, and treatment beds), every quarter; where a source is annual, the scorecard says so and prints a quarterly stand-in. In the same quarterly report: time to trial and downtown storefront vacancy.

Why: Downtown was left to get worse. Robberies in Victoria rose 19% in 2025 (Statistics Canada, Table 35-10-0184). In the Downtown Victoria Business Association's 2026 survey, 73% of respondents named drug use in public spaces as a top challenge, and retail vacancy downtown was 9.6%, down from 11% in 2025 (Victoria News, 20 Jun 2026). Drug policy, treatment beds and the courts are provincial decisions.

The link: businesses stay open downtown when people get treatment and housing and the street is safe. The business association is asking the Province for more detox and treatment beds and for services spread across the region (same source).

Who does what: drug law and bail are federal. Treatment, detox, supportive housing, involuntary care, prosecution, the courts and sheriffs are provincial. The City runs bylaws and, through its police board, VicPD (What the Province controls).

Staffing and funding: the party's 2024 costing put about $55 million (2025/26 and 2026/27) toward more sheriffs and judges, more front-line officers, addictions treatment in jails and a rapid trial court for minor offences, and about $38 million toward faster access to voluntary treatment and detox, with recovery communities in the capital plan (2024 costing appendix, Oct 2024). Beds need staff as well as buildings, so I'll vote for both the capital and the operating money (scorecard line 12). Where new staff or beds are needed, I won't invent a dollar figure; I'll publish the cost when Ministry or Island Health numbers exist.

Tracked on the scorecard: 5. Sleeping outside · 6. Toxic-drug deaths · 7. Crime in the city · 12. Treatment beds

Transport and BC Ferries

  • Reliability: a BC Ferries Charter that sets out service and performance expectations, with new leadership sought if they aren't met (2024 party platform, p. 112).
  • Maintenance: renew the aging fleet through open, competitive procurement, so fewer sailings are lost to breakdowns (2024 party platform, p. 113).
  • Executive accountability: tie part of executive pay to performance results (2024 party platform, p. 112).
  • Cancellation reporting: publish cancellations on the Victoria route by cause every quarter, so mechanical breakdowns are counted separately from weather (my position).
  • Fares, a separate measure: consult commuters and other frequent users on a monthly flat-fee program or other measures that give more certainty and less hassle (2024 party platform, p. 112).

What changes for you: fewer sailings between Swartz Bay and Tsawwassen lost to breakdowns, and more certainty on fares if you travel often.

How you'll know: from the reports BC Ferries already publishes, not from my 12-line scorecard. Reliability: cancellations by cause and on-time performance on Tsawwassen–Swartz Bay, in BC Ferries' quarterly Sailing Statistics (counted in sailings) and its annual report to the BC Ferries Commission (counted in round trips; the Coastal Ferry Act requires it within four months of each contract year). I'll link each new report in my quarterly report. Fares: the average fare increase the BC Ferries Commissioner allows, currently capped at 3.2% a year until March 2028 (Province of B.C.).

Why: in the fiscal year ended March 31, 2025, BC Ferries cancelled 295 round trips on Tsawwassen–Swartz Bay: 267.5 for vessel mechanical problems, 26 for weather and 1.5 for other reasons. On-time performance on the route was 77.6% (BC Ferries annual report to the BC Ferries Commission, year ended March 31, 2025). BC Ferries wrote that the issues that come with managing an aging fleet were evident that year (same report).

Since then: from April 1 to December 31, 2025, 38 sailings were cancelled on the route, 36 of them for weather and 2 for mechanical problems. From January 1 to March 31, 2026, another 105 sailings were cancelled, 101 of them for mechanical problems (BC Ferries Sailing Statistics, April 2025 – March 2026). For the whole fiscal year ended March 31, 2026, BC Ferries' annual report counts 71.5 cancelled round trips on the route (51.5 vessel mechanical, 20 weather) and on-time performance of 83.0% (annual report to the BC Ferries Commission, year ended March 31, 2026).

Sailings and round trips are different counts: a sailing is one crossing between two terminals; a round trip is the journey from the home terminal and back. The quarterly statistics count sailings and the annual report counts round trips, so 38 sailings can't be set against 295 round trips.

Preventable disruption: weather and emergencies will always cancel some sailings. Breakdowns are where maintenance, fleet renewal and management make the difference. The 2024 party platform described a fleet aging to the point where maintenance challenges routinely affect service (p. 113). That is where accountability belongs.

Who decides: BC Ferries is an independent company under the Coastal Ferry Act. The Province funds routes and service levels through the Coastal Ferry Services Contract, and the BC Ferries Commissioner regulates average fare increases (Province of B.C.). The 2024 platform also proposed pressing Ottawa for B.C.'s fair share of federal support for fleet renewal (p. 112).

Responsible spending

  • Publish a line-by-line register of government savings that anyone can check, and match new extra spending with a cut somewhere else (my position, scorecard line 2).
  • Protect front-line services: the party's 2024 costing said funding for front-line services, including health, education and public safety, would be maintained or increased (2024 party platform). I won't cut health care or education to write a slogan (my position).
  • Review government spending to find what isn't delivering results and move the money to priorities (2024 party platform: a core review called the Taxpayer Respect Audit).
  • Show what each commitment on this page costs, using the party's 2024 costing until it publishes a 2026 update (my position).
  • Vote against a bill that doesn't say what it cuts, or that raises a tax rate (my position, scorecard promise 2).

What changes for you: a provincial budget you can check line by line. On its own it doesn't put money in your pocket; it shows whether the tax relief on this page is paid for.

How you'll know: scorecard line 2, every quarter, from the government's Quarterly Reports, and each year from the Public Accounts.

Where things stand: the First Quarterly Report projects a $13.8 billion deficit for 2026-27 (B.C. Ministry of Finance, 14 Sep 2026). Budget 2026 books $3.5 billion of "expenditure management" savings over the fiscal plan (Budget and Fiscal Plan 2026/27, Table 1), but there is no public, line-by-line register a voter can check.

The route to balance: in 2024 the party committed to balance the budget in its second term, through faster economic growth, strategic new spending and moving wasteful spending to priority areas (2024 costing appendix, Oct 2024). Its platform said an immediate return to balance would require severe cuts to front-line services, which it would not make (2024 platform, p. 8). The party has not yet published a 2026 fiscal plan. I'll update this section when it does.

What the commitments cost (party estimates, Oct 2024): income-tax relief on housing costs, about $900 million in Budget 2026; the small-business tax cut to 1%, about $150 million in Budget 2026; the drivers' package, including no PST on affordable used cars, about $60 million from 2026/27; the public-safety package (more sheriffs, judges and front-line officers, among other items), about $55 million; the treatment package (faster access to voluntary treatment and detox), about $38 million (2024 costing appendix). Restoring the 5.06% rate and indexing has no published cost yet; its year and its offset follow the party's fiscal plan (scorecard line 1).

Also for this riding

  • James Bay: a cruise-day plan for traffic, buses and pedestrian safety, made with the City, the Harbour Authority and residents (my position). Ogden Point had 310 cruise ship calls in 2025 (Greater Victoria Harbour Authority, Oct 2025).
  • Public servants: almost half of B.C.'s public service works in Victoria (Government of B.C., figures as of June 2020). I'll push for published service standards, and for any change to be based on data, with staff told early (my position).

How I'll report to you

A number on every promise. A report every quarter.

Every quarter, I'll publish what was promised, what was done and what it cost. The scorecard comes out within 30 days of each quarter-end.
